Question:
A 56-year-old man has been having bloody bowel movements on and off for the past several weeks. He reports that the blood is bright red, it coats the outside of the stools, and he can see it in the toilet bowl even before he wipes himself. When he does so, there is also blood on the toilet paper. After further questioning, it is ascertained that he has been constipated for the past 2 months and that the caliber of the stools has changed. They are now pencil thin, rather than the usual diameter of an inch or so that was customary for him. He has no pain. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis? A: Anal fissure, B: Cancer of the cecum, C: Cancer of the rectum, D: External hemorrhoids
Reasoning:
1. Identify key clinical features:
This 56-year-old man presents with painless bright red blood per rectum (BRBPR) that coats the outside of the stool, is visible in the toilet bowl, and on wiping. He also reports chronic constipation and a change in stool caliber to pencil-thin stools over the past two months. He denies pain.
2. Gather Knowledge:
BRBPR and stool caliber change causes: Bright red blood per rectum (BRBPR) and a change in stool caliber (e.g., pencil-thin stools) are concerning signs that may point to an obstructing lesion in the distal colon or rectum, such as rectal cancer. While BRBPR can also be seen in benign anorectal conditions (e.g., hemorrhoids, anal fissures), the associated finding of stool caliber change is more specific for malignant or obstructive processes. These findings are not typical of irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), despite IBS occasionally causing altered bowel habits or perceived stool narrowing due to spasm. Therefore, in the context of chronic constipation, BRBPR, and narrowing stools, colorectal malignancy—especially rectal cancer—should be strongly suspected and further evaluated.
Colon cancer symptoms vs hemorrhoids vs anal fissure: Colon cancer, hemorrhoids, and anal fissures can all present with rectal bleeding, but they differ in key features. Colon cancer often causes painless bright red blood per rectum along with changes in stool caliber (such as pencil-thin stools), and may be associated with weight loss or other systemic symptoms—especially in older patients or those with a family history. Hemorrhoids typically cause painless bright red bleeding seen on the toilet paper or coating the stool, without any change in stool caliber or systemic signs. Anal fissures, on the other hand, usually cause painful defecation with minimal bright red bleeding on the surface of the stool or toilet paper, often triggered by constipation. These distinctions help guide further evaluation, especially in determining the need for colonoscopy to rule out malignancy.
Rectal cancer presentation: Rectal cancer commonly presents with bright red blood per rectum (hematochezia), change in bowel habits such as pencil-thin stools, tenesmus (a feeling of incomplete evacuation), mucus discharge, and constipation due to obstruction. These symptoms are more frequent when the tumor is located in the rectosigmoid region. A digital rectal exam can detect a fixed mass, and a thorough history—including family history—is essential. Diagnostic evaluation typically includes endoscopy for lesion localization and biopsy, along with imaging (CT, MRI, or transrectal ultrasound) to assess local invasion and metastasis. These clinical features distinguish rectal cancer from benign anorectal conditions and support early suspicion and evaluation.
Constipation and pencil-thin stool differential: Constipation and pencil-thin stools can arise from a variety of causes. Functional constipation, often seen in children during toilet training, involves voluntary stool withholding, leading to hard stools and painful defecation. In adults, constipation is defined by infrequent bowel movements, straining, hard stools, or incomplete evacuation. Causes include colonic obstruction (such as tumors), motility disorders, medications, and endocrine conditions like hypothyroidism and hyperparathyroidism. Pencil-thin stools, in particular, raise concern for obstructive processes in the distal colon or rectum, such as colorectal cancer. While irritable bowel syndrome can mimic some of these symptoms, the presence of persistent narrowing in stool caliber should prompt further evaluation for possible mechanical obstruction.
Bright red blood per rectum painless differential diagnosis: Painless bright red blood per rectum (BRBPR) can result from a variety of conditions, both benign and malignant. Common benign causes include hemorrhoids, diverticulosis, polyps, anal fissures, ulcers, abscesses, and proctitis. Hemorrhoids are perhaps the most frequent benign etiology, especially in younger patients. In older adults or those with concerning features (e.g., stool caliber change), malignancy such as rectal cancer must also be considered. Rectal cancer often presents with hematochezia, stool narrowing, mucus discharge, tenesmus, and obstructive symptoms, and may be painless in early stages. Massive painless BRBPR in the elderly may suggest diverticulosis or angiodysplasia, particularly when accompanied by hemodynamic instability.

### Response:
3. Compare each option to clinical information and retrieved knowledge:
Option A: Anal fissure – Typically causes painful defecation with small amounts of bright red blood on the toilet paper. It does not cause stool caliber changes or significant bleeding into the toilet bowl. This patient’s lack of pain and pencil-thin stools make this unlikely.
Option B: Cancer of the cecum – Usually presents with occult bleeding and iron-deficiency anemia, not bright red blood or changes in stool caliber. It’s located in the right colon, where stools are more liquid, so this is an unlikely match.
Option C: Cancer of the rectum – Commonly causes painless bright red blood per rectum, stool caliber changes, and constipation due to obstruction. The patient’s symptoms match this classic presentation, making it the most likely diagnosis.
Option D: External hemorrhoids – Can cause painless BRBPR, especially seen on wiping, but they do not cause pencil-thin stools or changes in bowel habits. This doesn’t explain the full picture in this case.
The answer is: C: Cancer of the rectum.

Question:
An 86-year-old man has become progressively unable to live independently for the past 10 years, and he now requires assistance with bathing, dressing, toileting, feeding, and transfers in and out of chairs and bed. On physical examination, he has no motor or sensory deficits. He cannot give the current date or state where he is. Six months later, he suddenly becomes comatose and dies. At autopsy, there is a large superficial left parietal lobe hemorrhage. Histologic examination of the brain shows numerous neocortical neuritic plaques and neurofibrillary tangles. The peripheral cerebral arteries and the core of each plaque stain positively with Congo red. Which of the following mechanisms is most likely responsible for his disease? A: Aggregation of Aβ peptide, B: Conformational change in the prion protein (PrP), C: Dopamine deficiency, D: Expansion of polyglutamine repeats
Reasoning:
1. Identify key clinical features:
An elderly man has experienced progressive decline in function over 10 years, culminating in full dependence for all activities of daily living. He also displays disorientation and memory loss, suggesting advanced dementia. On autopsy, there is a parietal lobe hemorrhage, numerous neocortical neuritic plaques, neurofibrillary tangles, and Congo red staining of plaques and cerebral vessels—classic findings of Alzheimer disease with associated cerebral amyloid angiopathy.
2. Gather Knowledge:
Alzheimer disease amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles: In Alzheimer disease, the characteristic histopathologic findings include amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques and neurofibrillary tangles composed of tau protein. While amyloid plaques are pathognomonic and help define the disease, the severity of dementia correlates more strongly with the distribution and amount of neurofibrillary tangles. These tangles typically begin in the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus and spread to other brain regions as the disease progresses. Congo red staining may highlight amyloid deposition, including in the cerebral vasculature—a finding associated with cerebral amyloid angiopathy, which can lead to lobar hemorrhages, particularly in elderly individuals. Thus, aggregation of Aβ peptide plays a central role in the disease's pathophysiology and complications.
Congo red staining in cerebral amyloid angiopathy: Congo red staining is a key histopathologic method used to diagnose cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), a condition characterized by the deposition of amyloid beta peptides in the walls of small- and medium-sized cortical and meningeal vessels. These deposits give vessels a rigid appearance and weaken the vessel walls, predisposing them to hemorrhages, especially lobar hemorrhages in the cerebral cortex. In addition to large symptomatic bleeds, CAA can also lead to microhemorrhages. While brain biopsy is rarely performed, when done (e.g., in suspected CAA-related inflammation), Congo red and beta-amyloid immunostaining help confirm the diagnosis by visualizing amyloid deposition. Thus, Congo red staining plays a definitive role in identifying vascular amyloid pathology associated with CAA.
Causes of parietal lobe hemorrhage in elderly with dementia: In elderly patients with dementia, parietal lobe hemorrhage is most commonly caused by cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA). CAA involves amyloid deposition in the walls of small- to medium-sized cerebral vessels, leading to arteriolar degeneration and increased risk of hemorrhage. This condition frequently causes lobar hemorrhages, particularly in the cortex, and can recur. Specifically, parietal lobe hemorrhages can present with hemisensory deficits and may occur without typical signs such as seizures or neck stiffness. Alzheimer disease, the most common form of dementia in the elderly, often coexists with CAA, and amyloid deposits may be found both in the parenchyma (as plaques) and in cerebral vessel walls, further increasing the risk of hemorrhage.

### Response:
3. Compare each option to clinical information and retrieved knowledge:
Option A: Aggregation of Aβ peptide – This is the hallmark of Alzheimer disease and cerebral amyloid angiopathy. Aβ accumulation forms neuritic plaques in the cortex and deposits in cerebral vessels, leading to Congo red positivity and increasing the risk for lobar hemorrhage—matching this patient’s dementia and fatal parietal hemorrhage.
Option B: Conformational change in the prion protein (PrP) – This describes prion diseases like Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease, which are rapidly progressive (weeks to months), not slowly progressive over 10 years like this case, and do not show amyloid plaques or Congo red positivity in vessels.
Option C: Dopamine deficiency – Associated with Parkinson disease, which features bradykinesia, rigidity, and resting tremor. While dementia can develop in Parkinson's, motor symptoms dominate early, which is not seen in this patient.
Option D: Expansion of polyglutamine repeats – Refers to Huntington disease, which presents with chorea and behavioral changes in midlife, not late-onset dementia with amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles.
The answer is: A: Aggregation of Aβ peptide.

Question:
An elderly house wife lost her husband who died suddenly of Myocardial infarction couple of years ago. They had been staying alone for almost a decade with infrequent visits from her son and grandchildren. About a week after the death she heard his voice clearly talking to her as he would in a routine manner from the next room. She went to check but saw nothing. Subsequently she often heard his voice conversing with her and she would also discuss her daily matters with him. This however, provoked anxiety and sadness of mood when she was preoccupied with his thought. She should be treated with: A: Clornipramine, B: Aiprazolam, C: Electroconvulsive therapy, D: Haloperidol
Reasoning:
1. Identify key clinical features:
An elderly woman, who recently lost her husband to a sudden myocardial infarction, had been living with him alone for nearly a decade with infrequent visits from her son and grandchildren. About a week after his death, she began clearly hearing his voice speaking to her from another room, despite no one being there. These auditory experiences continued, with her hearing him converse with her and responding by discussing her daily matters with him. These episodes trigger anxiety and sadness, particularly when she is preoccupied with thoughts of her deceased husband.
2. Gather Knowledge:
Grief-related hallucinations in elderly: Grief-related hallucinations, particularly auditory or visual, can occur as part of the normal grieving process in elderly individuals. These hallucinations are often benign, transient, and reflect the individual's yearning or preoccupation with the deceased. For example, a person may hear the voice of a lost loved one or briefly see them, especially in the early period following the death. While hallucinations can also suggest underlying psychiatric or neurologic conditions such as depression, early dementia, or sensory deprivation syndromes like Charles Bonnet syndrome, the context of recent bereavement and the emotional content of the hallucinations are key to identifying them as part of a normal grief reaction rather than a pathological process.
Management of persistent bereavement-related hallucinations: Management of persistent bereavement-related hallucinations depends on the context and severity of the symptoms. In cases where the hallucinations are part of persistent complex bereavement disorder—a condition marked by prolonged and impairing grief—the hallucinations may include transient perceptions of the deceased’s presence (e.g., hearing their voice or seeing them in familiar settings). While such experiences are not uncommon and can be normal early in grief, if they persist and significantly impair psychosocial functioning, they may warrant clinical attention. Treatment approaches can vary: supportive psychotherapy and grief counseling are first-line interventions. If hallucinations cause distress or functional impairment and resemble those seen in psychotic disorders, antipsychotic medications may be considered. In rare, treatment-resistant cases, clozapine may be used, though it requires close monitoring due to the risk of agranulocytosis. Importantly, differentiating grief-related hallucinations from hallucinations caused by primary psychotic or neurologic disorders is crucial before initiating pharmacologic treatment.
Pharmacologic treatment of elderly patients with hallucinations: Pharmacologic treatment of hallucinations in elderly patients must be carefully tailored to minimize risks, particularly due to increased sensitivity to antipsychotic medications in this population. In conditions like Lewy Body Dementia, where hallucinations are common and often distressing, atypical antipsychotics such as pimavanserin, quetiapine, clozapine, and aripiprazole may be used cautiously. However, these patients often exhibit neuroleptic sensitivity, making the use of standard antipsychotics hazardous. In Charles Bonnet Syndrome, where visual hallucinations occur in cognitively intact elderly individuals with visual impairment, low-dose atypical antipsychotics like quetiapine or olanzapine are sometimes used when hallucinations are persistent or disturbing, although efficacy varies. Overall, treatment typically begins with non-pharmacologic approaches, and medications are reserved for cases where hallucinations cause significant distress or functional impairment. Starting at low doses and closely monitoring for adverse effects such as sedation, confusion, or extrapyramidal symptoms is essential.
Haloperidol use in elderly psychosis: Haloperidol, a high-potency first-generation antipsychotic, is sometimes used at low doses to manage agitation or psychosis in elderly patients, particularly in acute settings like delirium. However, its use in dementia-related psychosis is associated with significant risks and carries an FDA boxed warning. Studies have shown an increased mortality rate—approximately 1.6 to 1.7 times higher—in elderly patients with dementia-related psychosis treated with antipsychotics, including haloperidol. Furthermore, it increases the risk of cerebrovascular adverse events such as stroke and transient ischemic attacks. Therefore, while haloperidol may be used cautiously for short-term management of severe agitation or behavioral disturbances, especially in delirium, it should generally be avoided for long-term treatment in elderly patients with dementia. When used, it requires close monitoring for adverse effects, and second-generation antipsychotics may be considered safer alternatives in some cases.

### Response:
3. Compare each option to clinical information and retrieved knowledge:
Option A: Clomipramine – This tricyclic antidepressant is primarily used for obsessive-compulsive disorder and depression, but it has significant anticholinergic side effects, especially problematic in the elderly. It is not indicated for transient grief-related hallucinations and would not address the primary issue in this case.
Option B: Alprazolam – A short-acting benzodiazepine used for acute anxiety. While this woman has grief-related sadness and anxiety, benzodiazepines are not first-line treatment for bereavement-related disturbances, especially in the elderly due to fall risk, sedation, and dependence.
Option C: Electroconvulsive therapy – ECT is used for severe, treatment-resistant depression or catatonia. This patient is experiencing transient, non-psychotic grief-related hallucinations and sadness—not the type of severe depression that would warrant ECT.
Option D: Haloperidol – This antipsychotic may be cautiously used in the elderly when hallucinations are distressing and persistent, especially if they impair functioning or resemble psychotic features. While it carries risks, in this context—ongoing hallucinatory conversations causing emotional distress—it is the most appropriate pharmacologic option if non-pharmacologic interventions fail.
The answer is: D: Haloperidol.
