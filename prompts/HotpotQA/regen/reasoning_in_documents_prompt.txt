### Response:
Infant botulism typically presents with a range of neuromuscular and autonomic symptoms. Common early signs include constipation, feeding difficulties, weak cry, and drooling. As the condition progresses, infants often exhibit acquired hypotonia, symmetrical descending weakness, weak sucking reflex, hoarse crying, and diminished gag reflex. Additional findings include marked head lag, ptosis, and shallow breathing due to respiratory muscle involvement. The classic presentation is often referred to as a "floppy baby". Due to the risk of rapid respiratory failure, prompt recognition and supportive care (including potential intubation) are critical.

### Response:
Botulinum toxin is a potent zinc-dependent neurotoxin that acts by binding to extracellular receptors on cholinergic nerve terminals. It enters the neuron via receptor-mediated endocytosis and cleaves SNARE proteins (e.g., SNAP-25, synaptobrevin, and syntaxin), which are essential for the docking and fusion of acetylcholine-containing vesicles at the presynaptic membrane. This cleavage results in the inhibition of acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction, causing a reversible blockade of both voluntary motor and autonomic cholinergic transmission. Clinical manifestations of this blockade include muscle weakness, dry mouth (xerostomia), blurred vision, diplopia, dysarthria, dysphonia, dysphagia, and, in severe cases, respiratory failure.

Chronic bronchitis, a subtype of COPD, involves chronic inflammation of the airways, particularly the bronchi and bronchioles. This inflammation leads to increased mucus production, bronchial wall thickening, and narrowing of airways. Mechanistically, increased parasympathetic (vagal) activity stimulates acetylcholine (ACh) release, which acts on muscarinic receptors of bronchial smooth muscle and glands, resulting in bronchoconstriction and mucus plugging.
